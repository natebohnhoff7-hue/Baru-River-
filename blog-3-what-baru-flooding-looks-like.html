What It Looks Like When the Barú Floods

River Soul · Dominical, Costa Rica · elbaru.com

October 2024. I went down to the crossing and the river was unrecognizable.

I've lived on the Barú. I know this river in every mood — low and glassy in dry season, brown and fast in rainy season, loud at night when it's been raining in the mountains. But that October I stood at the crossing and the road was gone. Not flooded like you'd ford it with a truck. Gone. The water was moving fast, carrying debris, and the whole thing — the road, the markers, the low concrete edge — was just underwater. You could not tell where the road ended and the river began.

That's what people don't understand until they see it. They imagine a puddle. A flooded street. Something manageable. The Barú doesn't do puddles.

When the watershed gets hit — and Tinamastes and Platanillo, the valleys upstream, can get hammered even when it's barely raining at the coast — the water arrives fast. Two to six hours from heavy upstream rain to a flooded crossing. By the time it looks threatening, it's already dangerous.

The color changes first. Clear blue-green to murky brown, then reddish. Then you start hearing rocks moving in the current, a sound like nothing you've heard before. Then the level starts climbing and it doesn't stop until the rain does — and the rain is somewhere you can't see.

Green season in Dominical runs May through November. That's six months where any serious rain event can put the Barú over. We had six confirmed flood events in 2025 alone. In October and November the ground is so saturated that even a moderate storm will push the river up.

This is the reality of living near or visiting the Barú. It's also why elbaru.com exists — so you know before you leave the house, not after you're standing at the edge of moving water.
